A year's income, decided on a guess.

The crop a smallholder plants in May determines what the household eats, earns and owes for the next twelve months. Almost none of that decision is made with numbers in front of it.

The decision

One choice, one chance, no undo.

Farming in Jigawa, Kaduna, Kano, Katsina, Kebbi, Sokoto and Zamfara runs on a single wet season. Land is committed once. Seed is bought once. Fertiliser is bought at whatever it costs that week. From planting to harvest is three to five months, and the result arrives all at once, at the end.

A farmer who picks wrong does not find out in time to correct it. There is no second planting to recover the loss, and often no buffer to absorb it the money that went into the ground was borrowed, or was the last of the previous harvest.

So the decision carries the weight of the whole year. And it is usually made with three things: what worked last season, what the neighbours are planting, and what someone heard a crop was selling for at a market some distance away.

The gap

Four things a farmer needs, and rarely has together.

Any one of these alone is misleading. It is having all four at once, for every crop under consideration, that turns a guess into a decision.

What it sells for

Not last year's price, and not the price in a market four hours away. The recent regional price for this crop.

What it yields

A realistic harvest for this region not the best case from a demonstration plot with inputs a smallholder cannot buy.

What it costs to grow

Seed, fertiliser, chemicals and labour, for one hectare. The number most often left out of the conversation entirely.

What is left over

Revenue minus cost. The only figure that actually answers the question being asked, and the one nobody publishes crop by crop.

What it costs

The highest price is not the best crop.

This is the trap that the missing cost figure sets. Take two crops from the survey data on this site, both grown on one hectare in the North-West.

Maize sells for ₦858 a kilo and yields well. Groundnut sells for nearly double that. But maize costs ₦990,666 a hectare to produce and groundnut costs ₦386,666 and once the bill is paid, groundnut leaves more behind.

A farmer comparing only prices, or only yields, cannot see that. A farmer comparing what is left over can.

The frustrating part

This information already exists.

Every year the National Agricultural Extension and Research Liaison Services surveys the wet season and publishes yields, production costs and market prices, broken down by zone. The North-West figures a farmer in Katsina needs are already collected, already checked, already public.

They are also, in practice, unreachable. The report is:

  • A long PDF, written for policymakers and researchers.
  • In English, in institutional prose, at a reading level that assumes a technical audience.
  • Organised by indicator, not by decision prices in one table, yields in another, costs in a third, with nothing that puts a single crop's four numbers together.
  • Silent on the actual question. It never subtracts cost from revenue, because that is not what the report is for.
  • Heavy to download on a phone, on a connection that is metered and intermittent.

The gap is not a gap in the data. It is a gap between the data and the person standing in the field in May.
